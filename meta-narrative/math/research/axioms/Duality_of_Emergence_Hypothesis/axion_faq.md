Minimal necessary and sufficient conditions for emergence:

Necessary: At least two distinguishable components, a non-trivial interaction, and a mechanism for generating system-level properties not reducible to the components alone.
Sufficient: The presence of such components and interactions, plus irreducibility of emergent properties to any subset or aggregation of the parts.
Counterexample: If properties are fully decomposable into parts, emergence is not present.
Structural and dynamical constraints for emergence:

Structural: The system must have distinguishable, interacting parts; homogeneity or lack of interaction precludes emergence.
Dynamical: The system’s evolution must allow for the generation of new, irreducible properties; static or trivially evolving systems do not exhibit emergence.
Both constraints must be met for genuine emergence.
Formalizing emergence as a falsifiable axiom:

Yes, emergence can be formalized: “Emergence occurs iff a system’s global properties cannot be deduced from any proper subset of its components and their interactions.”
Minimal structure: A set with at least two elements and a non-trivial relation.
Falsifiability: If a system’s properties are always reducible, the axiom is falsified.
Ontological and informational prerequisites for emergence:

Ontological: Existence of distinguishable entities and relations.
Informational: The system must encode more information at the global level than is present in the sum of its parts (synergy, mutual information, etc.).
Absence of these prerequisites precludes emergence.
Distinguishing emergence from aggregation or trivial composition:

Emergence is present only if system-level properties are irreducible and not present in any part or simple sum.
Aggregation/trivial composition: Properties are fully explainable by parts; no emergence.
Test: Remove or alter a part—if the property vanishes or fundamentally changes, it is emergent; if not, it is aggregate.
